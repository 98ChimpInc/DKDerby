How Scoring Works

Last updated: June 2026

DK Derby is built around one idea from psychology: the Dunning-Kruger effect, where people who know the least are often the most sure of themselves, and genuine experts tend to second-guess what they actually know. Every question in the app asks you to do two things ... rate how confident you are before you answer, then answer.

The stats below all flow from that single move. They measure the gap between what you think you know and what you actually know, and they show you, over time, which direction your gut leans.

Two scales run through everything:

  • Confidence is a 0–100 value you set on the slider, in steps of 5.
  • Competence / accuracy is a 0–100 value the quiz computes from whether you were right.

Confidence

How sure you said you were, on average ... your self-rated certainty before seeing whether you were right. You set it per question on the confidence slider (0–100, in steps of 5); the app averages those ratings.

average confidence = (sum of every per-question confidence) ÷ (number of questions answered)

How to read it: A high number means you tend to commit ... you back yourself. A low number means you hedge. Neither is “good” on its own; confidence only becomes meaningful when you line it up against how accurate you actually were (see Calibration gap). On a single day’s result it’s the average across that session’s questions; on the Stats screen it’s your lifetime average across every question you have ever answered.

Example

You rate three questions 80%, 60%, and 100%. Your confidence for that session is (80 + 60 + 100) ÷ 3 = 80%.

Competence / Accuracy

How well you actually did ... the percentage of questions you got right. The app uses the words “competence” and “accuracy” for the same underlying number: the share of your answers that were correct. The per-session result screen labels it COMPETENCE; the Stats screen labels it ACCURACY.

accuracy % = (correct answers ÷ questions answered) × 100

How to read it: This is the honest scoreboard ... it ignores how you felt and just counts right answers. 100% means you nailed everything; 0% means you missed everything. It’s deliberately shown right next to Confidence so you can see, at a glance, whether your certainty was earned.

Example

You answer 5 questions and get 3 right. Your accuracy is 3 ÷ 5 × 100 = 60%.

Naming note: “Competence” and “Accuracy” are the same value computed the same way. The two labels exist for tone ... “competence” pairs more naturally with “confidence” on the result card, while “accuracy” is the plainer word on the analytical Stats screen.

Calibration Gap (Points)

The heart of the app. Your calibration gap is the distance between how confident you were and how well you actually did, expressed in points on the same 0–100 scale. It is signed, and the sign is the whole point: a positive gap means you were overconfident (you climbed “Mount Stupid”), a negative gap means you were underconfident (the impostor zone), and zero means you were perfectly calibrated.

gap = confidence − competence

> 0  overconfident  < 0  underconfident  0  well-calibrated

How to read it: Read the sign first, the size second. +20 means “I was 20 points more sure than I deserved to be.” −20 means “I actually knew 20 points more than I gave myself credit for.” A gap near 0 in either direction means your confidence tracked reality closely ... that’s the skill the app is training.

Example

You felt 85% confident across a session but scored 60%. Your gap is 85 − 60 = +25 ... a strongly overconfident day.

If instead you’d felt 50% confident and scored 75%, your gap would be 50 − 75 = −25 ... you sold yourself short.

Signed gap vs. absolute gap ... two different questions

There are two honest ways to average gaps over many questions, and they answer different things:

  • Mean signed gap (confidence − competence, keeping the sign) = your directional bias. It tells you which way you lean overall ... chronically cocky, or chronically modest. Overconfident questions and underconfident ones partly cancel out, so a near-zero mean signed gap can mean “balanced” or “wildly swingy but even in both directions.”
  • Mean absolute gap (averaging |confidence − competence|) = your calibration tightness. It ignores direction and just measures how far off you were, question by question. A small absolute gap means your confidence reliably matches your accuracy; a large one means you’re often far off, regardless of which way.

Both are useful. The signed average tells you which way you’re wrong; the absolute average tells you how wrong you are. DK Derby standardises on the signed convention for the headline gap because, for a Dunning-Kruger app, the direction of your error is the more interesting story.

GAP · PTS Readout

The single-number gap shown on the day’s result card and the Home recap row, sitting beside CONFIDENCE and COMPETENCE. It’s the calibration gap for that one surface (a session, or your lifetime), rendered as a signed points value.

GAP = confidence − competence   (shown as +N, −N, or 0)

How to read it: It’s the same calibration gap described above, just the compact on-screen form. The “· PTS” suffix is the unit ... points on the 0–100 scale, not a percentage of anything. A + next to it is overconfidence; a − is underconfidence.

Example

A result card reading CONFIDENCE 80 · COMPETENCE 55 · GAP +25 is telling you that on that day you were 25 points more confident than correct.

Performance Badges

A single label that captures the shape of a result ... not just whether you did well, but how your confidence and competence combined. After each session the app picks exactly one badge by checking your accuracy and confidence against a fixed ladder of rules, top to bottom, and awarding the first one that fits.

Badge Condition What it means
Humble Genius accuracy ≥ 80 and confidence ≤ 70 You did great and stayed modest ... knew your stuff without showing off.
Grounded Learner accuracy ≥ 60 and confidence ≤ 80 Solid and sensible ... decent results, restrained confidence.
Mount Stupid Climber accuracy ≤ 50 and confidence ≥ 70 The classic Dunning-Kruger peak ... very sure, not very right.
Confidently Wrong accuracy < 40 and confidence ≥ 75 Even surer, even less right ... peak overconfidence.
Lucky Guesser accuracy > 70 and confidence < 50 You scored high while doubting yourself ... underconfident, possibly lucky.
Average Thinker none of the above The default when no specific pattern fits.

How to read it: The rules are checked in order, so the first matching condition wins. The badges aren’t a leaderboard; they’re a mirror. “Mount Stupid Climber” and “Confidently Wrong” sit on the overconfident end, “Lucky Guesser” sits on the underconfident end, “Humble Genius” and “Grounded Learner” are the well-calibrated middle, and “Average Thinker” catches everything in between. On the Stats screen these accumulate as collectable stamps, so you can see which persona you earn most often.

Example

A session with 45% accuracy and 85% confidence fails the Humble Genius and Grounded Learner checks, then matches Mount Stupid Climber (accuracy ≤ 50, confidence ≥ 70) ... so that’s the badge, even though it also satisfies Confidently Wrong’s confidence half.

Note: Two badge systems coexist. The per-session badge above is what you see on the result card and what accumulates as stamps. The Stats screen header also shows a separate, coarser “headline persona” derived from your lifetime counts. They share names but are computed differently; the per-session badge is the one most surfaces use.

Current & Longest Streak

Current streak

How many days in a row you’ve shown up ... the count of consecutive days, ending today (or yesterday), on which you finished the daily Question of the Day. A day “counts” only when a QOD session is actually completed; starting one isn’t enough.

A day is credited if you played it or it was bridged by a streak freeze. Only played days add to the count; frozen days bridge the gap without incrementing it. Your streak is alive as long as today or yesterday is credited. Miss a day with no freeze to cover it, and the run lapses to 0. Practice (non-QOD) quizzes don’t pad it ... only the daily question keeps a streak going.

Example

You play Mon, Tue, Wed, skip Thu (no freeze), then play Fri. On Friday your current streak is 1 ... the Thursday miss broke the Mon–Wed run, and Friday starts a fresh one.

Longest streak

Your personal best ... the longest run of consecutive credited days you have ever achieved, anywhere in your history. This only ever goes up or stays flat ... it’s the high-water mark, not your current run.

Example

You once played 12 days straight, then lapsed. Even with a current streak of 2 today, your longest streak still reads 12.

Streak Freezes

A streak freeze is a “skip day” ticket: spend one to bridge a missed day so your streak survives. A spent freeze doesn’t add to your streak count; it just keeps the existing run alive across the missed day.

How the allowance works

  • Free users: A small fixed lifetime allowance (one save, by default). It’s available from day one with no streak requirement. Once that’s spent, the app invites you to upgrade.
  • Premium users: One freeze per calendar week (Monday-based), no streak requirement, with a wallet cap of 4. Unused tickets bank up to that cap, so you can build a small buffer but not stockpile indefinitely.

Streak at risk

When you miss a day, the app can show you the streak a freeze could still rescue ... the consecutive run ending at your most recently played day. Once a miss has already dropped your current streak to 0, this value still holds the run a freeze would restore, so the offer can show you exactly what’s on the line.

Example

You played a 6-day run ending Wednesday, then missed Thursday. On Friday your current streak is 0, but your streak-at-risk is 6 ... and spending a freeze on Thursday would bring that 6-day run back to life.

30-Day Logbook

A strip of the last 30 days showing, for each day, whether you played and how calibrated you were ... your recent consistency at a glance. Each cell is one day, oldest on the left, today on the right.

For each of the last 30 days, the app looks at that day’s most recent finished daily session and classifies it:

  • Well-calibrated when |confidence − accuracy| ≤ 15
  • Played when you finished a session but the gap was larger than 15
  • Missed when no session finished that day

How to read it: This is your habit tracker plus a calibration heat-strip. A day shaded as well-calibrated means your confidence and accuracy were within 15 points of each other ... tight. A plain “played” day means you showed up but were further off. Tapping a day surfaces that session’s result.

Note: The logbook uses an absolute 15-point window, not the signed gap. For this strip the app only cares how close you were, not which direction you erred, so a +15 day and a −15 day are both “well-calibrated” here.

Per-Category Gaps (Category Insights)

Where your overconfidence and underconfidence actually live ... your calibration gap broken down by question category, so you can see, say, that you’re cocky about History but too modest about Science. Powers the “INSIGHT” footnote on Home.

avg gap = average of (confidence − 100 × isCorrect) per category, rounded to a whole number

Same signed convention as the overall gap: positive = overconfident, negative = underconfident. Categories with fewer than 3 answers are hidden (too little data to be meaningful), and the surviving categories are ranked by the size of the gap (largest first).

Example

With “History +18” and “Science −12” both above the 3-answer floor, the Home insight reads: “You’re most overconfident on History (avg gap +18 pts). You’re most underconfident on Science (avg gap −12 pts).”

Calibration Counters

Tallies of standout single answers ... how many times you’ve been notably overconfident, notably underconfident, or at the two extremes. These feed the lifetime persona and some badge logic. Each counter is checked per answered question:

  • Overconfident ... confidence ≥ 80 and the answer was wrong
  • Underconfident ... confidence ≤ 40 and the answer was right
  • Mount Stupid ... confidence ≥ 95 and the answer was wrong (extreme overconfidence)
  • Humble Genius ... confidence ≤ 25 and the answer was right (extreme underconfidence)

How to read it: “Overconfident” and “Mount Stupid” sit on the cocky side (sure but wrong); “Underconfident” and “Humble Genius” sit on the modest side (right but doubtful). The two extreme counts are stricter, near-the-edge versions of the milder counts. The app compares your overconfident vs. underconfident totals to decide your lifetime leaning persona.

Example

Answering a question wrong while 95% sure adds 1 to both your overconfident count (≥ 80, wrong) and your Mount Stupid count (≥ 95, wrong).

Lifetime Averages

Your all-time session scoreboard ... your average accuracy, confidence, and competence taken across every quiz session you’ve ever completed, plus the total number of sessions.

average quiz accuracy = (sum of each session's accuracy %) ÷ (number of sessions)
average quiz confidence = (sum of each session's avg confidence) ÷ (number of sessions)
average quiz competence = (sum of each session's competence %) ÷ (number of sessions)
total quiz sessions = count of every completed session

How to read it: These are session-weighted averages ... each session counts once, regardless of how many questions it held.

Note: The per-answer averages (Stats “OVERALL” block: AVG CONFIDENCE, ACCURACY, AVG GAP) weight each question equally, while these session averages weight each session equally. With sessions of differing lengths the two can differ slightly ... that’s expected, not a bug.

Analytics Tiers

Coarse, privacy-safe buckets the app uses internally to understand how its players are doing in aggregate. These are not raw numbers and are deliberately blurred so they can never single out an individual.

  • Calibration tier ... from gap = confidence − accuracy (signed): > 10 → overconfident, < −10 → underconfident, otherwise well-calibrated.
  • Streak bucket ... 0, 1–3, 4–7, 8–14, 15–30, or 31+.
  • Total-played bucket ... 0, 1–9, 10–49, 50–99, or 100+.

How to read it: These are segmentation labels, not scores you chase. The calibration tier is the same overconfident / well-calibrated / underconfident idea as everywhere else, just with a ±10-point dead zone around perfect.

Note: The calibration tier’s dead zone is ±10. This is a slightly different threshold from the Home calibration grade (which uses an absolute-gap ladder: under 10 = A, under 20 = B+, under 30 = B, under 40 = C, else D). Both describe “how calibrated are you,” but the analytics tier keeps the sign and uses a single ±10 cutoff, while the on-screen grade ignores sign and uses graduated bands.

Calibration vs. Accuracy ... Why This App Cares About the Gap

Accuracy answers “how much did you get right?” Calibration answers a deeper question: “did you know what you knew?” You can be highly accurate and badly calibrated (acing a quiz while feeling unsure ... the Lucky Guesser), or quite inaccurate and badly calibrated in the other direction (confidently bombing it ... the Mount Stupid Climber). The two are independent, and that independence is exactly what the Dunning-Kruger effect is about.

The original Dunning-Kruger finding wasn’t “dumb people are confident.” It was subtler: across many skills, the people who performed worst were the least able to recognise that they’d performed badly, because the very knowledge needed to do well is the same knowledge needed to judge your own performance. Confidence and competence drift apart most at the bottom. Meanwhile genuine experts often underestimate themselves, assuming what feels easy to them is easy for everyone.

DK Derby turns that into a game by separating the two scores and then measuring the gap between them. Accuracy alone would just be a trivia quiz. By asking you to commit to a confidence before you find out, and then showing you the signed gap, the app trains the rarer and more valuable skill: an honest sense of the edges of your own knowledge. A well-calibrated person isn’t necessarily the smartest in the room ... they’re the one who knows, in the moment, whether they’re right.

That’s the climb down from Mount Stupid, and the number that tracks it is your calibration gap.
